15 Planar Graphs, Euler's Formula, 5- and 6-Colour Theorems, Colouring, Connectivity

1. Why this topic matters

Planarity, colouring, and connectivity are central graph invariants. Euler's formula links vertices, edges, and faces; the 5-colour theorem is a classic induction proof; connectivity measures robustness. These results appear in map colouring, network reliability, and planar graph bounds.

2. Prerequisites

Graph basics, bipartite/Euler, and tree theorems from page 11, page 12, and page 13.

3. Intuition

A planar graph can be drawn without crossings. Euler's formula says for a connected plane graph, n−e+f=2, where the outer face is counted. The first picture is a planar embedding of K4 with 4 faces. Connectivity measures robustness: the second picture shows two copies of K4 sharing one vertex, where removing that single vertex disconnects the graph even though every non-shared vertex has degree 3.

4. Definitions

Planar graph: has a drawing without edge crossings.

Plane graph: a particular planar embedding.

Face: maximal region of the plane not used by the embedding; outer face counts.

Dual: vertices correspond to faces; edges cross original edges.

Chromatic number χ(G): minimum proper vertex colours.

Colour-critical graph: χ(G-v) < χ(G) for every vertex v.

Vertex connectivity κ(G): minimum vertices whose removal disconnects G.

Edge connectivity κ'(G): minimum edges whose removal disconnects G.

5. Theorems and complete proofs

Each proof is written in numbered steps. The connectivity theorem and colour-critical examples are labelled as standard results not present in the slides.

Theorem: Euler's formula

If a connected plane graph has n vertices, e edges, and f faces, then n−e+f=2. Exam likely.

  1. Base case. For n=1 the graph has e=0 and one (outer) face, so 1−0+1=2.
  2. Induction step. For n>1 choose an edge that is not a loop and contract it.
  3. Effect of contraction. Both n and e decrease by 1, while the faces on either side of the contracted edge merge into a single face; the total number of faces is unchanged.
  4. Apply induction. The smaller connected plane graph satisfies (n−1)−(e−1)+f=2, which simplifies to n−e+f=2. ∎

Theorem: planar edge bound

Every simple planar graph with n≥3 vertices has at most 3n−6 edges.

  1. Face lengths. In a simple plane graph with n≥3 each face boundary has length at least 3, so Σfaces length(F) ≥ 3f.
  2. Count edge-sides. Each edge borders two face-sides, so Σ length(F) = 2e; hence 2e ≥ 3f.
  3. Substitute Euler. f = 2−n+e, so 2e ≥ 3(2−n+e) = 6−3n+3e.
  4. Rearrange. This gives e ≤ 3n−6. For bipartite planar graphs each face has length at least 4, giving the stronger e ≤ 2n−4. ∎

Theorem: 6-colour theorem

Every planar graph is 6-colourable.

  1. Small degree. By the edge bound, a planar graph has a vertex v of degree at most 5 (if all degrees were ≥6 then 2e≥6n, contradicting e≤3n−6).
  2. Induct. Remove v; the remaining graph is planar and, by induction, colourable with 6 colours.
  3. Extend. At most 5 neighbours of v are coloured, so at least one of the 6 colours is free for v. ∎

Theorem: 5-colour theorem

Every planar graph is 5-colourable. Exam likely.

  1. Base. For n≤5 use a distinct colour for each vertex.
  2. Induction setup. For n>5 choose a vertex v of degree at most 5 and colour G−v by induction with colours 1,…,5.
  3. Easy case. If the neighbours of v use at most 4 colours, give v a missing colour.
  4. Hard case. Otherwise v has five neighbours v1,…,v5 in cyclic order coloured 1,…,5. Let G1,3 be the subgraph induced by colours 1 and 3.
  5. Kempe chain. If v1 and v3 lie in different components of G1,3, swap colours 1 and 3 in v1's component; colour 1 becomes free for v.
  6. Otherwise. A 1–3 path joins v1 to v3; with v this cycle separates v2 from v4 and v5, so v2 and v4 lie in different components of G2,4. Swapping colours 2 and 4 in v2's component frees colour 2 for v. ∎

Theorem: connectivity inequality

For a simple graph G, κ(G) ≤ κ′(G) ≤ δ(G). Exam likely (Q45). Not in slides - standard result.

  1. κ′ ≤ δ. Remove all edges incident with a vertex of minimum degree δ; that vertex becomes isolated, so the graph disconnects after at most δ edges.
  2. κ ≤ κ′. Let F be a minimum edge cut of size κ′, separating vertex sets A and B.
  3. Build a vertex cut. For each edge of F choose its endpoint in A; these endpoints form a set W with |W| ≤ |F| = κ′.
  4. Disconnect. Removing W leaves no edge of the original cut, so A\W and B are separated; hence κ ≤ |W| ≤ κ′. ∎

Example: strict inequality in connectivity

Take two copies of K4 sharing exactly one vertex s (the second picture in section 3). Exam likely (Q45).

  1. Degrees. The six non-shared vertices have degree 3 and s has degree 6, so δ=3.
  2. Vertex connectivity. Deleting s separates the two copies, so κ=1.
  3. Edge connectivity. Deleting the three edges from s into one copy isolates that copy, so κ′≤3; since δ=3 and κ′≥κ while no one or two edges can separate a K4 from s, κ′=3.
  4. Conclusion. κ=1 < κ′=3 = δ=3, so the inequalities can be strict.

Outerplanar graphs and duals

Outerplanar: all vertices on the boundary of the unbounded face. Dual: vertices are faces; face lengths are degrees in the dual; sum of face lengths = 2e.

6. Worked examples

Example 1 (easy): faces of a planar graph

For a connected plane graph with n=6 and e=9, Euler gives f = 2−n+e = 2−6+9 = 5, including the outer face. Check: 6−9+5 = 2.

Example 2 (easy): K5 is not planar

K5 has 5 vertices and C(5,2)=10 edges. A simple planar graph on 5 vertices satisfies e≤3·5−6=9; since 10>9, K5 cannot be planar.

Example 3 (easy): K3,3 is not planar

K3,3 is bipartite with 6 vertices and 3·3=9 edges. A simple bipartite planar graph satisfies e≤2n−4=2·6−4=8; since 9>8, it is non-planar.

Example 4 (medium): an edge bound

A simple planar graph on 4 vertices has at most 3·4−6 = 6 edges. This is attained by K4, whose planar drawing has 4 faces; Euler confirms 4−6+4 = 2. On 6 vertices the bound is 3·6−6 = 12.

Example 5 (medium): colour-critical graphs

Not in slides - standard result (Q46). Kn is n-colour-critical: deleting any vertex leaves Kn−1 with chromatic number n−1. An odd cycle is 3-colour-critical: deleting a vertex from C5 leaves the path P4, which is bipartite and hence 2-colourable.

Example 6 (medium): chromatic number of C5

An odd cycle cannot be 2-coloured: alternating two colours around an odd number of vertices forces the last edge to join equal colours. Three colours suffice (colour one vertex differently from its two neighbours), so χ(C5)=3; exhaustive search over colourings confirms this.

Example 7 (hard): connectivity of the figure-eight

Two copies of K4 sharing a vertex s have 7 vertices and 12 edges. Six vertices have degree 3 and s has degree 6, so δ=3. Removing s separates the graph, so κ=1; removing the three edges from s into one copy also separates it, and no smaller edge set does, so κ′=3. Brute-force enumeration of all vertex and edge subsets confirms κ=1 and κ′=3.

9. Common mistakes and exam traps

  1. Forgetting the outer face. Wrong: the planar drawing of K4 has 3 faces. Right: it has 4 — three bounded plus the outer face — and 4−6+4=2.
  2. Using the edge bound outside its range. Wrong: for n=2 the bound gives e≤3·2−6=0. Right: the bound requires n≥3; two vertices can have one edge.
  3. Confusing the 4- and 5-colour theorems. Wrong: the 5-colour theorem is best possible. Right: the 4-colour theorem is stronger but harder; this page proves the 5- and 6-colour results.
  4. Weakening colour-critical. Wrong: C5 is 3-critical because removing some vertex makes it 2-colourable. Right: removing every vertex must lower the chromatic number; each vertex of C5 leaves a path P4, which is bipartite.
  5. Confusing vertex and edge connectivity. Wrong: the figure-eight graph has κ=3. Right: deleting the shared vertex disconnects it, so κ=1, while κ′=δ=3.
  6. Using the wrong planarity bound. Wrong: K3,3 with 6 vertices and 9 edges satisfies 9≤3·6−6=12, so that bound does not rule it out. Right: it is bipartite, so use e≤2n−4=8, and 9>8.
  7. Reversing the connectivity inequality. Wrong: δ≤κ′≤κ. Right: κ≤κ′≤δ; for the figure-eight this reads 1≤3≤3.

10. Practice questions (assigned to this page)

Q45. Show κ(G) ≤ κ′(G) ≤ δ(G) for a simple graph G, and give an example where the inequalities are strict.

Not in slides - standard result.

Step 1 (κ′ ≤ δ). Delete all edges incident with a vertex of minimum degree δ; that vertex becomes isolated, so κ′ ≤ δ.

Step 2 (κ ≤ κ′). Let F be a minimum edge cut, |F|=κ′, separating A from B. Choose the endpoint in A of each edge of F; the chosen set W has at most κ′ vertices. Removing W destroys every edge of the cut, so A\W and B are separated; hence κ ≤ |W| ≤ κ′.

Step 3 (strict example). Take two copies of K4 sharing one vertex s. Six vertices have degree 3 and s has degree 6, so δ=3. Removing s disconnects the graph, giving κ=1. Removing the three edges from s into one copy disconnects it, and no smaller edge cut works, so κ′=3. Thus 1 < 3 = 3: the first inequality is strict. Brute force over all vertex and edge subsets confirms κ=1 and κ′=3.

Q46. Give two examples of colour-critical graphs.

Not in slides - standard result. Recall that G is colour-critical if χ(G−v) < χ(G) for every vertex v.

Example 1. Kn has χ=n. Deleting any vertex gives Kn−1 with χ=n−1, so Kn is n-colour-critical. For n=3, K3 has χ=3 and deleting a vertex leaves an edge with χ=2.

Example 2. An odd cycle C2k+1 has χ=3 (it is not bipartite). Deleting any vertex leaves a path on 2k vertices, which is bipartite with χ=2. Hence every odd cycle is 3-colour-critical; C5 in particular.

11. Extra self-test questions

1. Euler's formula for a plane tree on n vertices?

A tree has e=n−1, so f=2−n+(n−1)=1: only the outer face.

2. Maximum edges in a simple planar graph on 6 vertices?

By the bound e≤3n−6 = 3·6−6 = 12.

3. Is K4 planar?

Yes: the picture in section 3 draws it with n=4, e=6, f=4, and 4−6+4=2.

4. Chromatic number of C5?

χ(C5)=3: an odd cycle is not 2-colourable, and three colours work. Exhaustive checking of colourings confirms 3.

5. Vertex connectivity of Kn?

κ(Kn)=n−1: deleting the n−1 neighbours of any vertex isolates it, and fewer deletions leave the graph connected.

6. Show K5 is non-planar.

K5 has C(5,2)=10 edges, but a simple planar graph on 5 vertices has at most 3·5−6=9. Since 10>9, it cannot be planar.

7. Show K3,3 is non-planar.

K3,3 is bipartite with 6 vertices and 9 edges; a bipartite planar graph has e≤2n−4=8. Since 9>8, it is non-planar.

8. Connectivity numbers of two K4's sharing a vertex?

δ=3 (six vertices have degree 3), κ=1 (delete the shared vertex), κ′=3 (three edges into one copy). Verified by enumerating all vertex and edge subsets.

12. Cheat sheet

Definitions

  • Planar / plane graph: drawable without crossings / a particular crossing-free drawing.
  • Face: maximal region of the plane; the outer face is counted in Euler's formula.
  • Dual: vertices correspond to faces; each original edge is crossed by one dual edge.
  • Outerplanar: every vertex lies on the boundary of the outer face.
  • Chromatic number χ(G): least number of colours for a proper vertex colouring.
  • Colour-critical: χ(G−v) < χ(G) for every v.
  • κ, κ′, δ: vertex connectivity, edge connectivity, minimum degree.

Theorems

  • Euler: connected plane graph satisfies n−e+f=2.
  • Simple planar with n≥3: e≤3n−6; bipartite planar: e≤2n−4.
  • Every planar graph has a vertex of degree ≤5 and is 6-colourable and 5-colourable.
  • 5-colour proof uses a Kempe-chain colour swap on the 1–3 or 2–4 subgraph.
  • κ ≤ κ′ ≤ δ; strictness occurs in two K4's sharing a vertex (1 < 3 = 3).
  • Kn is n-colour-critical; odd cycles are 3-colour-critical. Not in slides - standard result.
