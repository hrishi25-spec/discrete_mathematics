14 Matchings: Hall's Theorem and Stable Marriage

1. Why this topic matters

Matchings model assignment problems: jobs to people, partners in marriage, and maximum independent edge sets. Hall's theorem gives a perfect characterization of when a bipartite graph has a matching saturating one side, and Gale-Shapley proves stable matchings always exist. These are classic exam theorems.

2. Prerequisites

Graph basics, bipartite graphs, and degrees from page 11 and page 12.

3. Intuition

A matching is a set of edges with no shared endpoints. In a bipartite job–people graph, a matching saturating all jobs means everyone gets a compatible job. Hall's condition says this is possible exactly when every subset S of jobs has at least as many qualified people N(S) as jobs in S. The first picture shows a matching of size 2; the second shows a failure: three jobs qualified for only two people.

4. Definitions

Matching: set of non-loop edges with no shared endpoints.

Saturated vertex: endpoint of a matching edge.

Perfect matching: matching saturating every vertex.

Maximum matching: largest possible size.

Stable matching: no rogue pair (two people who prefer each other over their partners).

Rogue pair: a man and woman who prefer each other to their assigned partners.

Neighbourhood N(S): the set of vertices adjacent to at least one vertex of S.

Alternating path: a path whose edges alternate between matching and non-matching edges; it is augmenting if it starts and ends at unsaturated vertices.

Complete matching: another name for a perfect matching, saturating every vertex.

Preference list: a ranking of all members of the opposite sex, used by the Gale–Shapley algorithm.

5. Theorems and complete proofs

Theorem: Hall's theorem

An X,Y-bipartite graph has a matching saturating X iff |N(S)| ≥ |S| for every S⊆X. Exam likely.

  1. Necessity. If S is saturated, each of its |S| vertices is matched to a distinct vertex of N(S), so |N(S)| ≥ |S|.
  2. Sufficiency by contrapositive. Assume a maximum matching M does not saturate X, and choose an unsaturated u∈X.
  3. Reachability. Let Z be the vertices reachable from u by M-alternating paths; set S=Z∩X and T=Z∩Y.
  4. M pairs T with S−{u}. Each y∈T is reached by an alternating path ending in a matching edge from some x∈S, x≠u; conversely each x∈S−{u} is reached through its matching edge from T. Hence |T|=|S|−1.
  5. T = N(S). If some y∈N(S) were outside T, take v∈S adjacent to y. The edge vy is not in M (all matched edges from S go into T), so the alternating path to v extends to y, contradiction.
  6. Conclusion. |N(S)| = |T| = |S|−1 < |S|, so Hall fails; by contrapositive, Hall's condition forces saturation of X. ∎

Theorem: stable marriage existence (Gale–Shapley)

For equal numbers of men and women with complete preference lists, the Gale–Shapley algorithm terminates with a stable matching. Exam likely.

  1. Proposals. Each man proposes to women in decreasing preference, never proposing twice to the same woman; a woman tentatively keeps her favourite proposal so far.
  2. Improvement. A woman only exchanges a current proposal for one she prefers, so her held partner improves monotonically; a man's list position only worsens.
  3. Termination. There are at most n2 possible man–woman pairs, so after finitely many proposals every woman holds exactly one and the algorithm stops.
  4. Stability. Suppose a rogue pair (m,w) existed with each preferring the other to their final partner. Since m prefers w, he proposed to her before settling; w would have kept him unless she later held someone she prefers — but then she prefers that someone to m, a contradiction. ∎

Theorem: Gale–Shapley produces man-optimal stable matching

Among all stable matchings, Gale–Shapley with men proposing gives each man his best possible partner. Not in slides - standard result.

  1. Suppose not. Assume some stable matching T gives a man m a partner he prefers to his Gale–Shapley partner; choose such an m whose rejection by w=T(m) happened earliest.
  2. Why w rejected m. At that moment w held a man m′ she prefers to m.
  3. m′ cannot prefer w to his partner in T. Otherwise (m′,w) would be a rogue pair in the stable matching T, since w is matched to m there.
  4. Earlier rejection. So m′ prefers his T-partner to w and must have proposed to her before proposing to w, meaning he was rejected earlier than m — contradicting the choice of m.
  5. Conclusion. No man can do better in any stable matching, so the Gale–Shapley matching is man-optimal. ∎

6. Worked examples

Example 1 (easy): Hall's condition holds

Let X={a,b}, Y={1,2} with edges a-1, a-2, b-1. For the full set S={a,b}, N(S)={1,2}, so |N(S)|=2=|S|; singletons clearly satisfy the condition. Hence a matching saturating X exists: a-2 and b-1. Brute-force enumeration confirms this matching of size 2.

Example 2 (easy): Hall's condition fails

Let X={a,b}, Y={1} with edges a-1 and b-1. For S={a,b}, N(S)={1}, so |N(S)|=1 < 2. No matching saturates X: the single edge available can cover only one of a and b.

Example 3 (easy): three jobs, two qualified people

Let X={a,b,c} and Y={1,2}, with every x∈X adjacent to both 1 and 2 (the second picture in section 3). For S={a,b,c}, |N(S)|=2 < 3, so Hall fails and no matching can saturate all three jobs.

Example 4 (medium): Gale–Shapley by hand

Men: m₁ ranks w₁>w₂, m₂ ranks w₁>w₂. Women: w₁ ranks m₂>m₁, w₂ ranks m₁>m₂. Step 1: both propose to w₁; she keeps m₂ and rejects m₁ (1 proposal each so far). Step 2: m₁ proposes to w₂, who accepts. Total 3 proposals, matching (m₂,w₁) and (m₁,w₂). It is stable: m₁ prefers w₁ but w₁ prefers m₂, and m₂ has his top choice.

Example 5 (medium): maximum matching in a path

In the path P₄ with edges 1-2, 2-3, 3-4, a matching cannot use both 1-2 and 2-3 (they share vertex 2). Choosing 1-2 and 3-4 gives size 2, and no third edge can be added, so the maximum matching has size 2.

Example 6 (medium): a star

In K1,3 every edge uses the centre, so at most one edge can be chosen. A maximum matching has size 1, and no perfect matching exists (the other two leaves stay unmatched).

Example 7 (hard): proposal bound and uniqueness

With n men and n women, each man proposes to at most n women, so the algorithm halts after at most n2 proposals: 4 for n=2, 16 for n=4. The resulting stable matching need not be unique: men-proposing gives the man-optimal one, women-proposing the woman-optimal one, and these can differ.

9. Common mistakes and exam traps

  1. Checking Hall only for singletons. Wrong: in the graph with X={a,b,c} and Y={1,2}, each singleton has |N|≥1, so a saturating matching exists. Right: take S={a,b,c}; |N(S)|=2 < 3, so no such matching exists.
  2. Maximum versus perfect matching. Wrong: the star K1,3 has a perfect matching of size 1. Right: its maximum matching has size 1, but a perfect matching would need to saturate all 4 vertices, which is impossible.
  3. Assuming the stable matching is unique. Wrong: Gale–Shapley gives "the" stable matching. Right: several can exist; men-proposing gives the man-optimal one and women-proposing the woman-optimal one.
  4. Ignoring the hypotheses of Gale–Shapley. Wrong: apply the algorithm with unequal numbers of men and women or incomplete lists and expect the same conclusion. Right: the version proved here assumes equal numbers and complete preference lists.
  5. Misidentifying a rogue pair. Wrong: a woman preferring a man to her husband is enough. Right: both must prefer each other to their assigned partners.
  6. Choosing matching edges that share a vertex. Wrong: in P4, edges 1-2 and 2-3 form a matching of size 2. Right: they share vertex 2; the maximum matching is {1-2, 3-4}.
  7. Overstating the proposal bound. Wrong: each man may propose n2 times. Right: he proposes to each woman at most once, so at most n times, and the total is at most n2.

11. Extra self-test questions

1. Check Hall's condition for X={a,b}, Y={1}, edges a-1, b-1.

For S={a,b}, N(S)={1} has size 1 < 2, so the condition fails and no matching saturates X.

2. Does every bipartite graph have a perfect matching?

No. The example in question 1 is bipartite but has no matching saturating X, by Hall's theorem. A perfect matching requires |X|=|Y| as well as Hall's condition.

3. Why does Gale–Shapley always terminate?

Each man proposes to each woman at most once, so with n men and n women there are at most n2 proposals; the process must stop. It stops with everyone matched because an unmatched man still has a woman to propose to.

4. Can a stable matching contain a rogue pair?

No. If (m,w) preferred each other to their partners, m would have proposed to w earlier; she would have kept him unless she held someone she prefers, contradicting her preference for m.

5. Maximum matching in K1,3?

Size 1: every edge uses the centre, so two edges cannot coexist. There is no perfect matching, since three leaves cannot all be matched.

6. Maximum matching in the path P4?

Size 2, realised by edges 1-2 and 3-4. Edges 1-2 and 2-3 share vertex 2 and cannot both be used.

7. Does X={a,b}, Y={1,2} with edges a-1, a-2, b-1 satisfy Hall?

Yes: the singletons have at least one neighbour, and S={a,b} has N(S)={1,2} of size 2. A saturating matching is a-2 with b-1.

8. Run Gale–Shapley for m₁: w₁>w₂, m₂: w₁>w₂, w₁: m₂>m₁, w₂: m₁>m₂.

Proposal 1: m₁→w₁, proposal 2: m₂→w₁; w₁ keeps m₂ and rejects m₁. Proposal 3: m₁→w₂, accepted. The stable matching is (m₂,w₁), (m₁,w₂), reached in 3 ≤ 2² proposals.

12. Cheat sheet

Definitions

  • Matching: non-loop edges with no shared endpoints.
  • Saturated: incident to a matching edge; perfect: saturates every vertex.
  • Maximum matching: one with the largest possible number of edges.
  • N(S): vertices adjacent to at least one vertex of S.
  • Alternating path: edges alternate in/out of the matching; augmenting: starts and ends unsaturated.
  • Rogue pair: two people who prefer each other to their assigned partners; a matching without one is stable.

Theorems

  • Hall: a matching saturating X exists iff |N(S)| ≥ |S| for every S⊆X.
  • Hall's proof: an unsaturated u gives alternating-reachable sets with |N(S)|=|S|−1.
  • Gale–Shapley: men propose in preference order, women keep their best; terminates after at most n2 proposals in a stable matching.
  • Men-proposing Gale–Shapley gives the man-optimal stable matching (standard result).
  • A tree has at most one perfect matching; any matching on m edges saturates 2m vertices.

Exam checklist

  1. Check Hall for all subsets, not only singletons.
  2. To prove Hall, use the alternating-path sets S and T.
  3. In Gale–Shapley, list proposals in order and check stability via rogue pairs.
  4. State which side proposes when discussing optimality.
  5. Distinguish maximum size from perfect coverage.
