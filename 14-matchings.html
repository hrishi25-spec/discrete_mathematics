14 Matchings: Hall's Theorem and Stable Marriage

1. Why this topic matters

Matchings model assignment problems: jobs to people, partners in marriage, and maximum independent edge sets. Hall's theorem gives a perfect characterization of when a bipartite graph has a matching saturating one side, and Gale-Shapley proves stable matchings always exist. These are classic exam theorems.

2. Prerequisites

Graph basics, bipartite graphs, and degrees from page 11 and page 12.

3. Intuition

A matching is a set of edges with no shared endpoints. In a bipartite job-people graph, a matching saturating all jobs means everyone gets a compatible job. Hall's condition says this is possible exactly when every subset of jobs has at least as many qualified people as jobs. The picture below shows a matching of size 2.

4. Definitions

Matching: set of non-loop edges with no shared endpoints.

Saturated vertex: endpoint of a matching edge.

Perfect matching: matching saturating every vertex.

Maximum matching: largest possible size.

Stable matching: no rogue pair (two people who prefer each other over their partners).

Rogue pair: a man and woman who prefer each other to their assigned partners.

5. Theorems and complete proofs

Theorem: Hall's theorem

An X,Y-bipartite graph has a matching saturating X iff |N(S)|≥|S| for all S⊆X. Exam likely.

Proof. Necessity: vertices matched to S lie in N(S), so |N(S)|≥|S|. Sufficiency by contrapositive: if maximum matching M does not saturate X, let u be unsaturated in X. Let S be X-vertices reachable by M-alternating paths from u, and T the Y-vertices reached. Then M matches T with S-{u}, so |T|=|S|-1. Also N(S)=T, hence |N(S)|=|S|-1 < |S|. ∎

Theorem: stable marriage existence (Gale-Shapley)

For equal numbers of men and women with preference lists, the Gale-Shapley algorithm terminates with a stable matching. Exam likely.

Proof. Each man proposes to women in decreasing preference; women tentatively accept best proposal so far. A woman never loses a proposal without gaining a better one, and each man proposes at most once to each woman, so termination occurs. If an unmatched rogue pair (m,w) existed, m would have proposed to w before his final partner, and w would have accepted him unless she already had someone she prefers; contradiction. ∎

Theorem: Gale-Shapley produces man-optimal stable matching

Among all stable matchings, Gale-Shapley (men proposing) gives each man his best possible partner.

Proof. By induction on proposals: a woman never rejects a man who could be her partner in some stable matching unless she has a better stable partner. ∎

6. Worked examples

Example 1

Bipartite graph X={a,b}, Y={1,2} with edges a-1,a-2,b-1. Hall fails for S={a,b}: N(S)={1,2}? Actually |N|=2≥2, so matching exists. One matching: a-2, b-1.

Example 2

Hall fails example: X={a,b}, Y={1} with edges a-1,b-1. S={a,b}, N(S)={1}, size 1<2, no matching saturating X.

Example 3

Gale-Shapley with 2 men and 2 women terminates after at most 2 proposals per man; stable matching exists.

9. Common mistakes and exam traps

  • Checking Hall's condition only for singletons, not all subsets.
  • Confusing maximum matching with perfect matching.
  • Assuming stable matching is unique (it is not; man-optimal vs woman-optimal).
  • Forgetting Gale-Shapley requires equal numbers and complete preference lists.

11. Extra self-test questions

1. Hall's condition for X={a,b}, Y={1}, edges a-1,b-1?

Fails; no saturating matching.

2. Does every bipartite graph have a perfect matching?

No; Hall's condition may fail.

3. Gale-Shapley always terminates?

Yes.

4. Stable matching may have rogue pair?

No, by construction.

5. Maximum matching in star K1,3?

1.

12. Cheat sheet

Hall: matching saturating X iff |N(S)|≥|S| for all S⊆X.

Gale-Shapley always yields stable matching.

Men-proposing is man-optimal.

Matching edges share no endpoints.

Perfect matching saturates all vertices.
