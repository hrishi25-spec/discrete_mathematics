09 Pigeonhole Principle

1. Why this topic matters

The pigeonhole principle is a simple but powerful counting/contradiction tool: if more objects than boxes, some box has at least two. It proves non-injectivity, guarantees collisions, and underlies the birthday paradox. Many exam problems are one-line applications once you identify the pigeons and holes.

2. Prerequisites

Basic counting from page 02; inclusion-exclusion for collision counts from page 04.

3. Intuition

If 6 pigeons go into 5 holes, at least one hole has at least 2 pigeons. The proof is by contradiction: if every hole had at most 1, there would be at most 5 pigeons. The first picture shows 6 balls in 5 boxes. The second picture shows the generalized form: 10 = 3·3+1 objects in 3 boxes force at least 3+1 = 4 in one box, since three boxes holding at most 3 each could hold only 9.

4. Definitions

Pigeonhole principle: if n+1 objects are placed in n boxes, some box contains at least 2 objects.

Generalized form: if nk+1 objects are placed in n boxes, some box contains at least k+1 objects.

Empty box form: if n-1 objects are placed in n boxes, some box is empty.

5. Theorems and complete proofs

Every proof below identifies the pigeons, the holes, and the contradiction; the collision estimate keeps the approximation steps explicit.

Theorem: basic pigeonhole principle

If n+1 pigeons are placed in n holes, one hole has more than 1 pigeon. Exam likely.

  1. Suppose the contrary. Assume every hole holds at most one pigeon.
  2. Bound the total. With n holes, that gives at most n·1 = n pigeons.
  3. Contradiction. But there are n+1 pigeons, so the assumption is false and some hole holds at least two. ∎

Theorem: generalized pigeonhole principle

If nk+1 objects are placed in n boxes, some box has at least k+1 objects.

  1. Suppose every box has at most k objects. Then the total number of objects is at most n·k.
  2. Contradiction. The actual total is nk+1 > nk, so some box contains k+1 or more. ∎

Theorem: empty-box form

If n−1 objects are placed in n boxes, some box is empty.

  1. Suppose every box is nonempty. Then each of the n boxes holds at least one object, so there are at least n objects.
  2. Contradiction. Only n−1 objects were placed, so at least one box must be empty. ∎

Theorem: function non-injectivity

A function from a set of size at least k+1 to a set of size k is not one-to-one.

  1. Assign boxes. Let the k elements of the codomain be holes, and let the k+1 (or more) inputs be pigeons; each input is placed in the hole that is its image.
  2. Apply the basic principle. Some image receives at least two distinct inputs, so two inputs share a value and the function is not injective. ∎

Theorem: birthday collision approximation

For t random hashes into N=2n values, the probability of no collision is approximately exp(−t2/(2N)); setting it to 1/e gives t≈√(2N) = √2·2n/2. Exam likely. Not in slides - standard result.

  1. Compute the no-collision probability exactly. The first hash is unrestricted; the i-th must avoid the i−1 earlier values, which has probability 1−(i−1)/N. Hence Pno = ∏i=0t−1 (1−i/N).
  2. Linearise the exponential. For small i/N, 1−i/N ≈ exp(−i/N), so Pno ≈ exp(−Σi=0t−1 i/N).
  3. Sum the arithmetic progression. Σi=0t−1 i = t(t−1)/2 ≈ t2/2, giving Pno ≈ exp(−t2/(2N)).
  4. Solve for the 1/e threshold. exp(−t2/(2N)) = 1/e forces t2/(2N)=1, so t=√(2N) = √2·2n/2. ∎

6. Worked examples

Example 1 (easy): first letters

Pigeons = 27 English words; holes = the 26 letters of the alphabet. Since 27 > 26, two words must begin with the same letter.

Example 2 (easy): exam scores

Scores run from 0 to 100, so there are 101 possible scores (holes). With 101 students an injective assignment is still possible, but with 102 students the basic principle forces two students to share a score. Hence 102 is the minimum guarantee.

Example 3 (easy): the generalized form

Place 10 = 3·3+1 objects into 3 boxes. If every box held at most 3, there would be at most 3·3 = 9 objects, contradicting 10. Hence some box holds at least 4.

Example 4 (medium): the party problem

With n≥2 people, the possible numbers of acquaintances are 0,1,…,n−1. But 0 and n−1 cannot both occur: if someone knows everybody, nobody knows zero people. So there are only n−1 usable counts (holes) for n people (pigeons), and two people know the same number of others.

Example 5 (medium): six people

Fix a person A. Among the other 5, A either knows at least 3 or does not know at least 3 (the hint in the lecture notes). Suppose A knows B, C, D. If any two of B, C, D know each other, those two together with A form three mutual friends; if no two of them know each other, then B, C, D are three mutual strangers. The "does not know" case is identical with friends and strangers exchanged. Hence every group of 6 contains three mutual friends or three mutual strangers (the standard notation is R(3,3)=6, Not in slides - standard result.).

Example 6 (medium–hard): sums divisible by 100

Given 100 whole numbers a1,…,a100, form the partial sums sj = a1+…+aj modulo 100 for j=1,…,100. If some sj≡0, the first j numbers have sum divisible by 100. Otherwise the 100 sums occupy only the 99 nonzero residues, so two are equal, say su=sv with u<v; then au+1+…+av ≡ 0.

Example 7 (hard): a collision threshold

For a 10-bit hash, N=210=1024 and the estimate gives t≈√2·25≈45.25. Direct computation at t=45 gives Pno = ∏i=044(1−i/1024) ≈ 0.3749, while exp(−452/(2·1024)) ≈ 0.3720 — close to 1/e≈0.3679, confirming the approximation.

The triangle argument shows how choosing the holes makes a geometric statement immediate. Subdivide a side-2 equilateral triangle into four side-1 triangles; five points placed inside must put two in the same small triangle, whose diameter is 1.

9. Common mistakes and exam traps

  1. Choosing the wrong holes. Wrong: for 27 English words, use 27 letters as holes and conclude nothing. Right: there are 26 letters, and 27>26 forces two words with the same initial.
  2. Using the generalized form with the wrong k. Wrong: 100 objects in 10 boxes force a box with 11 (that would need 10·10+1=101 objects). Right: with 100 = 10·10 objects, if every box had at most 9 the total would be at most 90, so some box has at least 10.
  3. Confusing 1/e non-collision with a 1/2 collision chance. Wrong: t=√(2N)=45.25 for N=210 gives a 50% collision chance. Right: 45.25 gives collision probability ≈0.632; 50% needs t≈√(2N ln 2)≈37.68.
  4. Expecting a construction. Wrong: the 27-words argument identifies which two words share a letter. Right: the principle proves existence only; finding the pair needs inspection.
  5. Off-by-one for a shared exam score. Wrong: 101 students guarantee a repeated score among 0–100. Right: there are 101 scores, so 102 students are needed.
  6. Misstating the empty-box form. Wrong: 3 objects in 3 boxes force an empty box. Right: 2 objects in 3 boxes force one; with 3 objects each box can hold one.
  7. Halving the exponent in the hash formula incorrectly. Wrong: for 32-bit hashes, t≈√2·232≈6.07·109. Right: t≈√2·216≈92682.

10. Practice questions (assigned to this page)

Q16. A hash function produces n-bit values. Approximately how many hashes must be computed for a 1/e chance of no collision?

Not in slides - standard result. The approximation uses the exponential estimate and the hinted approximations t! ≈ (t/e)^t and t^(t−k) ≈ 1+kt for k ≪ t.

Step 1. There are N = 2n possible hash values. The probability that all t hashes are distinct is Pno = ∏i=0t−1 (1−i/N).

Step 2. For i/N small, 1−i/N ≈ exp(−i/N), so Pno ≈ exp(−Σi=0t−1 i/N) = exp(−t(t−1)/(2N)) ≈ exp(−t2/(2N)).

Step 3. Set Pno = 1/e: then t2/(2N) = 1, so t = √(2N) = √2·2n/2.

Step 4. For n=10, t ≈ √2·25 ≈ 45.25. Direct computation with N=1024 gives Pno≈0.3749 at t=45, while the formula gives exp(−452/2048)≈0.3720 — both near 1/e≈0.3679.

Second reading. If the question asked for a 1/2 chance of collision instead, solve exp(−t2/(2N))=1/2 to get t≈√(2N ln 2); for N=1024 this is about 37.68, not 45.25.

11. Extra self-test questions

1. If 10 balls go into 9 boxes, one box has at least how many?

Here n=9 holes and n+1=10 pigeons. If every box had at most 1 there would be at most 9 balls, so some box has at least 2.

2. If 100 objects go into 10 boxes, one box has at least how many?

If every box had at most 9, there would be at most 10·9=90 objects, fewer than 100. Hence some box has at least 10.

3. Five points in an equilateral triangle of side 2: two are within distance 1?

Divide the triangle into four side-1 triangles by joining the midpoints. Five points in four small triangles force two into one, and that triangle has diameter 1, so their distance is at most 1. With half-open small triangles (boundary points assigned to one piece) the distance is strictly less than 1. Not in slides - standard result.

4. Among 30 English words, must two share a first letter?

Yes. The 26 letters are holes and the 30 words are pigeons; since 30>26, two words begin with the same letter.

5. Birthday threshold for a 32-bit hash?

N=232, so t≈√2·216 = 1.414·65536 ≈ 92682 hashes give a 1/e chance of no collision.

6. In any 27 English words, two share an initial — why?

27 words (pigeons) into 26 letters (holes): if all initials differed, there could be at most 26 words, contradicting 27.

7. In any group of 6 people, three are mutual friends or mutual strangers.

Fix person A. Of the other five, A knows at least three or is unknown to at least three. Suppose A knows B, C, D. If any two of B, C, D know each other, that pair plus A are three mutual friends; otherwise B, C, D are three mutual strangers. The other case is the same with the roles exchanged.

8. From 100 whole numbers, choose one divisible by 100 or several summing to one.

Form partial sums sj modulo 100. If some sj≡0, the first j numbers work. Otherwise 100 sums lie in the 99 nonzero residues, so two coincide; the difference of the corresponding prefix sums is a sum of a consecutive block divisible by 100.

12. Cheat sheet

Definitions and forms

  • Basic: n+1 objects in n boxes ⇒ some box holds at least 2.
  • Generalized: nk+1 objects in n boxes ⇒ some box holds at least k+1.
  • Empty box: n−1 objects in n boxes ⇒ some box is empty.
  • Functions: a map from a set of size k+1 to one of size k is not injective.
  • Method: name the pigeons, name the holes, then apply the inequality or argue by contraposition.

Collision estimates

  • N = 2n possible hash values; Pno = ∏i=0t−1 (1−i/N).
  • Pno ≈ exp(−t2/(2N)); for Pno=1/e, t≈√(2N) = √2·2n/2.
  • For a 1/2 collision probability, t≈√(2N ln 2), which is smaller than √(2N).

Exam checklist

  1. Identify the pigeons and the holes explicitly.
  2. Check the strict inequality (> rather than ≥) before applying the principle.
  3. For a "same number of acquaintances" problem, exclude the incompatible endpoints 0 and n−1.
  4. For divisibility, use partial sums and compare residues.
  5. State that the principle proves existence, not a construction.
